# Proxy mode

The idea belongs to someone else — a business unit, leadership, a client, a colleague — and the user is deciding whether to commit their team to it. They defend on the owner's behalf.

This is the common case inside an organisation, and it changes what the grill is *for*. In standard mode the output is a verdict on an idea the user owns. Here the output is **the set of questions to take back to the person who does own it**, before eight weeks of a team's time is spent.

## What changes

**The steelman is confirmed differently.** Ask *"Is that a fair statement of what they are asking for?"* — not *"is that your argument?"* The user is not being asked to endorse it, only to represent it accurately. If they cannot, that is the first finding.

**"I don't know" is a correct answer.** In standard mode it is a crack in the user's thinking. Here it is often the only honest response, and it is exactly what you are mining for. Log it as an **owner question**, name who has to answer it, and move on. Never press a user on something only the requester could know — that punishes them for someone else's gap and teaches them to invent answers.

**Press only where the user genuinely has standing.** They can reason about feasibility, blast radius, cost, sequencing, what it breaks, and whether the stated problem matches the requested solution. They cannot reason about the requester's business case, budget, or private motive. Aiming there produces guesses, and guesses are worse than blanks.

**The ledger gains a third outcome.** Alongside `held` and `cracked`: `owner-question` — a point the user could not settle and that the requester must.

## Channel lenses — where the request came from tells you how it fails

Ask early, in one line: *"Where did this come from?"* Requests fail differently depending on their origin, and knowing the origin tells you which blind spot to check first. This does not replace the lens catalogue — it tells you which lens to open with.

| It came from | What that channel characteristically hides | Open here |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales, or one named customer** | one account's need generalised into a product | "How many others have asked for this? What happens to it when that customer leaves?" |
| **Leadership or strategy** | a solution that arrived before the problem — from a conference, a competitor, a board slide | "Describe the problem this solves for a user, in their words rather than in the deck's." |
| **Compliance or legal** | one interpretation of a rule presented as the rule | "What exactly is mandated, and what is one reading of it? What is the smallest thing that satisfies it?" |
| **Support or operations** | a symptom fix for a cause upstream | "What is causing these tickets? If that were fixed, would this request still exist?" |
| **Another product team** | a dependency framed as a favour | "What do they do if you say no? Who owns this interface in a year?" |
| **Data or analytics** | a metric mistaken for a goal | "What decision changes depending on what this shows? If none, why are we building it?" |
| **The user's own team** | consensus mistaken for validation | "Who outside this room has said they need this?" |
| **A regulator, auditor, or a signed contract** | the merit question is genuinely closed | Do not grill the merit. Establish the true minimum, and hand the rest to `risk-interrogate`. |

That last row matters more than it looks. When a request is genuinely mandatory, grilling whether it is a good idea wastes the user's time and makes the skill look naive. Say so, narrow the scope to what is actually required, and move on.

**Stay on merit.** These lenses ask whether the request holds up. Security posture, privacy compliance, cost at scale, and monitoring belong to `risk-interrogate` — the skill that runs once the decision is made. Raising them here produces questions the user cannot act on yet and buries the ones they can.

## Closing in proxy mode

Replace the standard close with:

### The request at its strongest
The steelman as confirmed, in one or two sentences. This is what the user takes into the meeting so the conversation does not start with a misunderstanding.

### What holds without them
The parts the user could defend unaided. These do not need the requester's time — say so explicitly, because the value of the meeting depends on not spending it on settled ground.

### Questions for the owner
Grouped by who must answer. Each with why it matters — a question with a stated cost is answered; a bare question is deferred.

| Question | Who answers | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| … | … | what goes wrong if it stays open |

### What you would be committing to
One or two lines: the scope, dependency, or risk the user's team inherits if this proceeds as written. This is the sentence that most often changes the meeting.

### Verdict
Same four labels, but they describe **the request**, not the user's reasoning. `UNRESOLVED` is the normal and expected outcome here — it means the meeting has a purpose. Say that plainly rather than presenting it as a failure.

## Do not

- Do not draft the user's talking points or write the meeting invite. Producing questions is this skill's job; running the meeting is theirs.
- Do not soften a question because it will be awkward to ask. An awkward question asked in week one is a cheap question.
- Do not let the user drift into defending the request as their own. If they start doing that, name it — *"you are arguing their case now; do you actually believe it?"* — because that drift is how teams end up owning decisions they never made.
