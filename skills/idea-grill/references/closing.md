# Closing the grill

The close is built from the **defense ledger** — the running record of which provocations held, cracked, or went unanswered. Write it from that record, not from your overall impression of the conversation. Impression drifts toward whoever spoke last and most confidently.

## Format

### Surviving thesis
The idea as it stands **after** the grill, in one or two sentences. This is almost never the opening thesis — it is usually narrower, more conditional, or aimed at a different problem. State it as the user would have to state it now, using their own words where they earned them.

### Cracks
Every `cracked` or `unanswered` entry. Three columns, and the middle one is the part most grills skip:

| Crack | Strongest answer available | What would close it |
|---|---|---|
| … | the defense a world-class operator would have given here | the specific evidence or decision |

The middle column is not a favour. Someone who has just been grilled for eight turns has learned that their argument was weak; without this, they have not learned what strong looks like. Give the real answer, including the ones the user never found — especially those.

"What would close it" must be specific enough to act on tonight. "Needs data" is useless; "the actual churn rate for accounts under ten seats" is a task.

### Fatal flaw
Only if one exists. A defect no defense addressed and that no reasonable amount of work removes. Do not manufacture one to look rigorous, and do not soften one to be kind.

### How the idea evolved
Two or three lines tracing what changed under pressure: what was dropped, what narrowed, what got sharper, and what never moved at all. This is often the most useful part of the output — it shows the user their own reasoning in motion.

### Verdict
One of the four below, with a single sentence of justification.

### Defense ledger
The full record, in order. It is what makes the verdict auditable rather than a mood.

| # | Provocation (lens) | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | … | held / cracked / unanswered |

## Verdicts

| Verdict | Meaning | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| **SURVIVES** | Held under pressure. Remaining cracks are minor and addressable. | Proceed. Shape it into a request. |
| **SURVIVES, NARROWED** | Everything load-bearing held, but only in a reduced scope, for a narrower audience, or under conditions absent from the original claim. | Proceed with the narrowed version explicitly written down — not the original one. |
| **UNRESOLVED** | A load-bearing premise went unanswered. The idea is neither dead nor defensible yet. | Name the missing evidence or decision, then re-grill once it exists. |
| **FATAL FLAW** | A defect surfaced that no defense addressed. | Stop. Kill it here, where it is cheap. |

**The line between NARROWED and UNRESOLVED** is load-bearing versus not. A premise is load-bearing when the idea cannot exist at all without it — supply, demand, distribution, legality. If one of those is unanswered, the verdict is UNRESOLVED no matter how much else held. If every load-bearing premise held and the idea merely shrank, it is NARROWED.

## Rules

- **Never inflate the verdict to be encouraging.** "SURVIVES" for an idea with an unanswered load-bearing premise is a lie that costs the user months.
- **Never manufacture a fatal flaw to look rigorous.** If it held, say it held, and name which attacks it survived — that is the most useful thing you can hand someone walking into a funding conversation.
- **Quote the user.** Their own strongest sentence persuades them more than your paraphrase of it.
- **Name the cheapest next step.** One line: the smallest piece of evidence that moves the verdict.
- **Then offer to save.** "Want me to save this to a file?" — and write it only on a yes.
